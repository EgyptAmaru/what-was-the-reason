What Was the Reason?

A social reasoning game. Think out loud together.

How to play

The idea. This is a social reasoning game. The theme is math, but math is only the material: underneath, every question is about thinking clearly. It is built to do two things at once, bond a group by getting them to think out loud together and dissolve the fear of being wrong. Many questions are designed so your gut answers wrong. That is the point, not a trap. When a reveal catches everyone, the right reaction is "we all fell for it," never "you got it wrong."

Who plays

Teams and host. Split into two teams and pick one host. Teams of 3 to 4 (about 8 people) work best, but the game scales, so play with more or fewer and it runs the same. The host runs the board, keeps time, and decides whether an answer counts.

Calculators. Allowed for arithmetic and nothing else. Nobody should feel shut out for not being quick at mental math.

The board

Reading the board. Four columns by six rows. Each column is a way of reasoning, not a school subject. Each row is a difficulty level, worth more points as you go down. Every square shows only its point value until it is opened, so you cannot pre-judge a question and steer around it.

Three acts. Rows unlock in pairs, and the whole room moves down together: clear both rows of an act to open the next.

  • Act 1 · Rows 1 and 2 · Solo. Players answer on their own, with generous time. Teams alternate questions. A safe warm-up where being wrong costs nothing.
  • Act 2 · Rows 3 and 4 · Team discussion. The picking team thinks out loud together while the other team stays quiet and listens.
  • Act 3 · Rows 5 and 6 · Simultaneous. Both teams work the same question during one shared window, lock in an answer with their reasoning, and reveal at the same time.

Taking turns. Picks alternate between teams and rotate through teammates, and whoever picks does not have to answer. In acts 2 and 3, one rotating spokesperson delivers the team's committed answer, so the spotlight spreads. A wrong answer belongs to the whole team, never the person who voiced it.

Wrong answers. No answer ever loses points. In acts 1 and 2, a miss offers the question to the other team as an optional steal; in act 2 the stealing team may use what they overheard. If nobody gets it, the host reveals the answer and the question retires. A question nobody solves still teaches something.

Scoring. An answer either meets the question's criteria or it does not, and reasoning that holds can win even without finishing the math. In act 3, when both teams reason well, both score, and a team that also shows the math beats one that only reasons out loud.

The host

Judging and guiding. Because a person judges answers against a range or rubric, questions do not need one exact machine-checkable answer, good reasoning is enough. The host's real job is the room: keep it constructive, read each answer generously, and frame the hard questions so a wrong guess feels expected. Setting up the surprise ahead of time ("your brain is supposed to get this one wrong") lets the reveal land as a shared laugh.

Host console (optional). The host can open the game on a second device, like a phone, alongside the main screen. It privately shows the host the hints, notes, and answer for whatever question is open, and can control the board remotely: open a question, flip to the answer, pick winners, and run the timer. It is entirely optional, the game plays fully from the main screen alone, and the host can read notes from a printout instead. To pair, tap "Host console" on this screen and scan the code with the phone.
